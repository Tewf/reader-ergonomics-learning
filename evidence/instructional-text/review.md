# Review

This file reads the literature on the apparatus of an explanatory study text, strand by strand: existing reviews, the seminal works, one strand per sub-question, then practice and Q/A sites.
Levels: **MA** meta-analysis, **SR** systematic review, **R** review without pooling, **E** experiment, **C** correlational or before-and-after study, **D** descriptive count, **A** argument. "(secondary)" marks a number read in an abstract, not in the paper. Grey quality scores are in [sources](sources.md); a source under 10 of 20 is named "example only".

## 1. Seminal works

- Hartley 1978, a book read through its ERIC record (grey tier 1): guidelines for writers and typographers, with texts shown before and after revision. Hartley 1977 gives fifty guidelines (abstract) in three groups (prose, tables and graphs, typography).
- Mager 1962 (grey tier 1, ERIC record): a programmed text that teaches to state objectives as observable behaviour, with conditions and a criterion of success.
- Biggs 1996: **constructive alignment**, objectives, teaching and assessment built on the same verbs, illustrated with one teacher-education unit (abstract).
- Cohen 1987: **instructional alignment** is claimed to give larger effects at lower cost than other constructs (abstract; the effect sizes were not read).
- Krathwohl 2002 (title and venue only) presents the revised taxonomy of objectives; Wiggins 2005 names **backward design** (book not opened; example only).

## 2. Pedagogical aids: prevalence, use, effect (Q1)

| Source | Finding | Level |
|---|---|---|
| Marek 1999 | counts aids in introductory psychology texts of 1995-1997; students rate 15 aids, extending Weiten 1996 (abstract) | D |
| Weiten 1999 | university, college and high-school students agree: bold terms, glossaries, chapter summaries and self-tests are most useful (abstract) | D |
| Gurung 2003 | more than 200 students: bold terms and practice questions used most; reported use and helpfulness do not relate to exam scores; rated helpfulness of key terms relates to worse scores (secondary) | C |
| Gurung 2004 | 240 students: use of aids not positively related to any exam; a poor first exam raised summary use only a little (secondary) | C |
| Gurung 2011; Landrum 2012 | perceived quality of aids, visuals and writing predicted how much was read and exam scores, varying by book; the link to grades is indirect (secondary) | C |
| Gurung 2012 | textbook use against quiz scores across 35 institutions: books differed in rated quality, not in quiz scores; grade point average and rated helpfulness predicted scores (abstract) | C |
| Benbassat 2008 | 230 respondents say boxed text is rarely studied; in a test with 177 readers, boxed and plain text were skimmed alike (secondary) | D; E |

- No experiment was found that removes one aid from a real textbook and measures learning.

|---|---|---|

## 4. Summaries, key terms and glossaries (Q3)

| Source | Finding | Level |
|---|---|---|
| Hartley 1979; Cook 1981 | an end summary gave better recall than one at the start of the same page, or none; in Cook, 40 students, an opening summary on a separate page did as well (abstracts) | E |
| Mayer 1996 | three experiments, 163 students: a summary of captions and simple drawings matched or beat the full text on recall and transfer (abstract) | E |
| Reder 1980 | studying only a summary beat reading the whole chapter on true-or-false statements of main points and inferences, 75.2% against 65.3% (Experiments 1 and 2, N = 63), at 20 minutes and at a week. The gap closed when the text had three times the study time, and was not significant after 6 to 12 months; a summary studied first did not hurt learning related new material (read in full, pp. 125-132) | E |
| van der Meij 2012 | numbered summaries inside the text against a structured abstract: recall d = 1.57, 40 students (secondary) | E |
| Yanagisawa 2020 | 42 studies, N = 3,802: glossed reading taught 45.3% of words at once and 33.4% later, against 26.6% and 19.8%; in-text glosses and glossaries were the least effective forms (secondary) | MA |
| Abraham 2008 | 11 studies: computer glosses had a medium effect on comprehension and a large one on incidental vocabulary (secondary) | MA |
| Stewart 1991 | marginal glosses in first-language college reading may be of limited use (abstract) | E |

- All gloss meta-analyses are in second-language reading, and no study of an end glossary in a first-language study text was found; defining terms before use is measured in the core (pre-training, Mayer 2002).

## 6. Guided notes, study guides, crib sheets (Q5)

| Source | Finding | Level |
|---|---|---|
| Larwin 2013 | 12 studies, 27 effects, 1,529 students: d = 0.55 (CI 0.34 to 0.75); 0.78 against students' own notes, 0.27 against the instructor's full notes (read, pp. 51-52) | MA |
| Larwin 2012 | school and special education, 11 studies, 412 students: d = 1.13, effects from -2.21 to 10.39; on exam scores d = -0.75 (read, Results) | MA |
| Konrad 2009 | guided notes raise note accuracy and performance, most for school-age students (abstract); its college part held three studies (Larwin 2013) | MA |
| Biggers 2020 | 22 adult studies: positive perceptions; gains in some domains, most with complex content; delivery mode made no difference (abstract) | SR |
| Dickson 2005b | a required textbook study guide raised multiple-choice scores; completing three quarters did no better than a quarter (abstract) | E |
| Horner 2007; Cushen 2019 | instructor-made guides did no better than students' own (Horner); a provided concept list lowered exam scores (Cushen) (abstracts) | E |
| Saleh 2026; Lieu 2017 | required guides beat optional ones (169 against 86 students); completing reading guides correlated with exam scores (abstracts) | C |
| Dickson 2008; Funk 2011; Burns 2014 | making a crib sheet did not teach; expecting to use one fostered dependency; use rose among weaker students (abstracts) | E; C |
| Hamouda 2016 | crib-sheet content tracked scores only on comprehension-level questions (abstract) | C |

- Duchastel 1983 models the ideal study guide by its functions, with no test (abstract).

## 7. Explained solutions (Q7)

- Wittwer 2008 (R): instructional explanations often fail to add learning; it gives design guidelines (abstract).
- Wittwer 2010 (MA, 21 experiments): explanations help conceptual more than procedural knowledge and do no better than self-explaining (abstract).
- Richey 2013 (E, three experiments): withholding stepwise explanations gave more conceptual learning; Gerjets 2006 found explanations ineffective (abstracts).
- Mendiolea 2013 (D, six students): learners valued detail, explanation and choice in worked solutions and skipped extension tasks (abstract).
- Access to solutions and its effect on homework is reviewed in the exercise-sets review (Rystrom 2005; MathEd 2021a; MathEd 2022b).

Q/A sites (Academia, Mathematics Educators, Computer Science Educators; 14 threads, 4 under 10 of 20; strings in [protocol](protocol.md)). Practitioner opinion, no data.

- Notes handed out: some students disengage, others check their notes (MathEd 2014h); one lecturer says provided notes rarely raise exam results (Academia 2017c).
- Writing one's own notes: to choose and omit what a long textbook holds (MathEd 2014i; Academia 2014a).
- Reading the exposition: students use exercises and answers but not the explanation; guiding questions and self-explanation training are offered (MathEd 2014k).
- Misconceptions: analyse the learner's wrong rule in front of the class (MathEd 2017b).
- Crib sheets: making one is said to focus study, which the experiments of strand 6 do not support; a handwritten card stops copying (MathEd 2017c; Academia 2018b). Study guides are best written by the learners (CSEd 2018c).
- Objectives: SMART criteria and Bloom's verbs (Academia 2017b). Glossary place and marking (Academia 2018c; Academia 2022a) and chapter length (MathEd 2014l), slides against notes (MathEd 2014j): example only.
