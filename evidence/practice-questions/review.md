# Review

This file reads the literature on questions written into study material, by strand: existing reviews first, then one strand per sub-question, then practice and Q/A sites. Terms are defined in [frame](frame.md). "(secondary)" marks a number read in an abstract or a summary, not in the paper; grey scores are in [sources](sources.md).

- How this review differs: each review above takes one part (level, format, item, feedback, generation); this one reads them as parts of one artefact, a question in a study text.
- It keeps learning outcomes apart from assessment quality: item-writing reviews measure tests, testing-effect reviews measure learning, and only Butler 2018 joins the two (read through a news release, strand 3).

## 1. Q1: kinds and levels of question

- Agarwal 2019: middle-school and college students; higher-order and mixed quizzes raised delayed higher-order test scores, fact quizzes did not (abstract).
- Jensen 2014: two sections of one biology course, a semester of low-level or high-level quizzes and exams; the high-level section scored higher on both low-level and high-level final items (abstract).
- McDaniel 2013 (middle school): application quiz questions raised exam scores on application and on definition items. Collins 2018 (college): application quizzes raised application items and matched factual quizzes on definitions (abstracts).
- Butler 2010 and Rohrer 2010: repeated testing improved new inferential questions a week later, and transfer map questions a day later in children (abstracts).
- Pan 2018 moderators: transfer grows with response congruency (d = 0.28 without, 0.58 with), elaborated retrieval practice and initial test accuracy (full text).
- Self-explanation and error-finding questions are core results (Bisra 2018; Alemdag 2025); no study here compared them with application questions inside one text.
- Field record of what questions ask: 93% of 9,713 items in 77 introductory biology courses were at Bloom levels 1 and 2 (Momsen 2010, full text). Also: introductory biology and physics sequences mostly assess lower-order skills (Momsen 2013, abstract).
- Dávila and Talanquer (Davila 2009): end-of-chapter items in general chemistry textbooks are mostly at application and analysis level, but narrow: algorithmic problems and predictions (abstract).

## 3. Q3: how an item is written

- Haladyna 1989: a taxonomy of 43 rules drawn from 46 textbooks; 96 studies reviewed; for nearly half the rules no research was found (abstract).
- Haladyna 2002: 31 guidelines for classroom assessment, from 27 textbooks and 27 studies since 1990 (abstract); Frey 2005: 40 rules on which 20 classroom-assessment textbooks agree (abstract).
- Rodriguez 2005: three options are optimal, by 80 years of research on psychometric quality; more items fit in the same time (abstract).
- Downing 2005: four medical-school exams; 36% to 65% of items flawed; flawed items 0 to 15 points harder; little effect on reliability (abstract).
- Tarrant 2006: 46.2% of 2,770 nursing items flawed, over 90% at low level, 36.2% from test banks; Tarrant 2008: 47.3% flawed, and high achievers, not borderline students, were penalised (abstracts).
- Moore 2026: 1,230 items in three domains; confusion-inducing flaws raised response time by 14% to 29%; cue-giving flaws showed no effect (abstract).
- Pachai 2015: "none of the above" made an item harder only as the key; replacing a good distractor with it lowered discrimination (abstract).
- Distractors from misconceptions: Sadler 1998 ranks children's astronomy ideas on one scale from distractor-driven items (abstract). Gierl 2017 finds distractor writing studied far less than stems (abstract); Little 2014 is the one learning result on distractor quality (strand 2).
- Butler 2018, as reported by its university (WashU 2018): three plausible options, items most students can answer, no "all" or "none of the above", no complex formats, then review of answers.

## 4. Q4: feedback content and timing

- Content, meta-analyses: high-information feedback d = 0.99, corrective 0.46, reinforcement or punishment 0.24 (Wisniewski 2020, Table 3). On items, EF 0.49, KCR 0.32, KR 0.05, and EF more effective for higher-order outcomes (Van der Kleij 2015, secondary). EF most likely best for lower- and higher-order outcomes, KR worst (Mertens 2022, secondary).
- Bangert-Drowns 1991: effect 0.28 over 40 effects, as Hattie 2007 lists it in Table 1 (read). Its own abstract gives 40 reports and 58 effects; feedback corrected errors when learners were led to attend to it (abstract).
- Single studies: explanation feedback equalled correct-answer feedback on repeated questions and beat it on new inference questions (Butler 2013). Also: correct answer after an error raised final retention by 494% in word learning, after a correct answer it changed little (Pashler 2005).
- Against more content: immediate explanation feedback on demanding adjunct questions cut time and accuracy on the next questions, then learning (Roelle 2016, N = 59). Also: the correct answer alone gave higher later performance than more complex feedback, in Kulhavy, White, Topp, Chan and Adams (1985), on reading passages with multiple-choice items, as Hattie 2007 reports it (read).
- Timing: classroom studies favoured immediate feedback, lab studies of test content delayed (Kulik 1988, abstract); with a 1988 to 2024 pool, timing made no average difference, g = 0.03 [-0.08, 0.13] (Kandemir 2026, abstract).
- Single studies of timing: delayed beat immediate on multiple-choice tests, and AUC equalled standard feedback (Butler 2007). Delayed beat immediate for children, not for adults once the lag to test was equated (Metcalfe 2009). Delayed elaborated feedback did not beat immediate (Candel 2021, N = 133). Yet 95.33% of 599 learners chose immediate feedback (Lefevre 2016).
- Classroom moderator: corrective feedback after quizzes g = 0.537 against 0.374 without (Yang 2021, full text).

## 5. Q5: where answers sit, and peeking

- Kulhavy 1977: feedback does not help retention when it is highly available before the learner responds (abstract).
- Anderson 1971: learners given the correct response right after answering learned more than those given none or those who could peek before answering (abstract).
- Covert answering is allowed by the evidence only if retrieval happens (Smith 2013b), and it is weaker for definitions (Rivers 2025): an answer visible on the same line would make retrieval optional (an inference).
- van den Broek 2023 controls availability within an item: the stem before the options; Brom 2026 found no gain in the wild.
- No study was found that compares answers folded beside the question, at the end of the chapter, and at the back of the book.

## 6. Q7: generated questions

| Source | Comparison | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Zhang 2016 | machine-generated, web-collected and textbook biology questions | rated pedagogy similar; knowledge-base questions shallower (abstract) |
| Van Campenhout 2021 | automatic and human-authored questions in one course | equal engagement, difficulty and persistence, by the vendor's authors (abstract) |
| Johnson 2024 | 5.2 million sessions, 800,000 generated questions, 9,000 textbooks | features that predict student ratings (abstract) |
| Lu 2021b | 91 students, generated short-answer practice against a control | practice group learned more; not compared with written questions (abstract) |
| Laupichler 2023 | 161 students, 25 human and 21 ChatGPT items | equal difficulty; discrimination 0.36 against 0.24 (abstract) |
| Law 2025 | 24 doctors, 100 and 100 items | AI items easier (0.78 against 0.69), similar discrimination, more inaccuracies, lower-order; 24.5 against 96 person-hours (abstract) |
| Chauhan 2025 | 120 students, crossover | 9 ChatGPT items against 19 faculty items met ideal criteria; nonfunctional distractors 33.75% against 13.75% (abstract) |
| Kuusemets 2024 | GPT-4 against an expert, vectors | the suggested key often needs correction (abstract) |
| Isley 2026 | 91 classes, nearly 1,700 students | refined AI items comparable to expert items by item response theory (abstract) |
| Elkins 2023; Scaria 2024 | teacher ratings; expert ratings by Bloom level | judged useful; automated evaluation not on par with human (abstracts) |

- Kurdi 2020 found little work on difficulty control, feedback generation and presentation, and asked for harmonised evaluation; Gorgun 2024 found no standard evaluation framework.
- No study found here measured learning from generated questions against learning from written ones.

## 8. Q/A sites

Mathematics Educators, Computer Science Educators and Academia Stack Exchange, searched 2026-10-29: 15 threads, none under 10 of 20. Practitioner opinion; no thread carries data.

- Q3 is what teachers ask most: where plausible wrong answers come from.
  - An item writer builds distractors from grade-level misconceptions, intermediate steps and calculation slips (MathEd 2020p); a teacher collects short answers one year and turns the common ones into options the next (MathEd 2017p).
  - Three or four options are argued from Haladyna 2002 and Rodriguez 2005, or from guessing odds (MathEd 2018p; Academia 2016q).
  - Wording: negation in a stem confuses (MathEd 2025p); extra context suits homework, not exams (Academia 2018p).
  - Writing good items is slow; teachers reuse banks and add 10 to 15 items a year (CSEd 2018p; Academia 2021p).
- Q2: teachers prefer free response for partial credit and for seeing reasoning, and use multiple choice for grading time (Academia 2016p; Academia 2021p). Asked for research, answerers found little on learning (MathEd 2016p; MathEd 2014p).
- Q4: explanation shown only after a correct choice invites gaming by retrying (CSEd 2024p). Q5: answers left out of books, or printed only for odd-numbered problems, are explained by grading and reuse of problems, not by learning (MathEd 2014q; MathEd 2017q).
- Q1: Bloom's verbs are judged a poor fit for mathematics (MathEd 2016q).
