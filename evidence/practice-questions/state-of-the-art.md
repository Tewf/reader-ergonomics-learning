# State of the art, per sub-question

This file names, for each sub-question, who holds the record and how it was measured. Two records are kept apart: **most adopted** (what study material and quiz tools do) and **best evidence** (what a study measured). Terms are defined in [frame](frame.md); every source is in [review](review.md).

## Best evidence

| Sub-question | Best evidence | Measured how | Measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 does a question transfer to new questions? | Pan 2018 | meta-analysis, 192 effects, N = 10,382 | d = 0.40 overall; 0.32 to application and inference questions |
| Q1 does a higher-order question help lower-order learning? | Jensen 2014; McDaniel 2013; Agarwal 2019 | one course over a semester; middle-school classes; lab and classes | yes for application and high-level questions; fact questions did not raise higher-order scores |
| Q2 recall or recognition? | Yang 2021 (classroom); Rowland 2014 (lab) | meta-analyses | classroom: recall 0.541, recognition 0.607 with feedback; lab: cued recall 0.61, recognition 0.29 |
| Q2 written or in the head? | Yu 2025 | meta-analysis, 18 studies, 2,560 participants (secondary) | covert g = 0.23; overt better by g = 0.17 |
| Q2 can a multiple-choice item induce retrieval? | Little 2012; Little 2014; van den Broek 2023 | lab experiments | competitive options teach the facts behind the wrong options; stem shown before options helps vocabulary |
| Q3 how many options? | Rodriguez 2005 | meta-analysis, 80 years | three, for psychometric quality, not measured on learning |
| Q3 what do flaws cost? | Downing 2005; Tarrant 2008; Moore 2026 | item analysis of real exams | 0 to 15 points harder; high achievers penalised; confusing flaws add 14% to 29% response time |
| Q4 what should feedback say? | Wisniewski 2020; Mertens 2022; Butler 2013 | meta-analysis, 435 studies; network meta-analysis, 77 studies; two experiments | more information, larger effect (high-information d = 0.99); explanation helps new inference questions |
| Q4 when should it come? | Kandemir 2026 | meta-analysis, 51 studies, 160 effects (secondary) | no average difference, g = 0.03 |
| Q5 does peeking cost? | Anderson 1971; Kulhavy 1977 | programmed instruction experiment; review | learners who could peek learned less (secondary) |
| Q7 are generated items as good? | Isley 2026; Laupichler 2023; Law 2025 | item response theory in 91 classes; item analysis | comparable after refinement; lower discrimination or easier without it |

## Upper bounds (achieved)

- Testing transfers to new application and inference questions at a literature level (Pan 2018).
- A semester of high-level questions raised low-level scores too, in one course (Jensen 2014).
- Multiple choice with competitive options matched cued recall and added learning about the wrong options (Little 2012).
- Explanation feedback beat correct-answer feedback on new inference questions (Butler 2013).
- Refined generated items reached expert items' psychometric quality in a field study (Isley 2026).

## Lower bounds (not yet shown)

- This search found no experiment comparing answers folded beside the question, at the chapter end and at the back of the book.
- It found no study of learning, as opposed to item quality, from generated questions against written ones.
- It found no study of learning as a function of the number of options; Rodriguez 2005 measures tests.
- It found no study testing an item-writing guideline for its effect on learning, except distractor competitiveness (Little 2014) and lures (Roediger 2005).
- Q/A sites (searched 2026-10-29) hold no data on any sub-question.
