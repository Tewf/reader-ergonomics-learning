# Against: null results, controversies and limits

This file gathers the evidence against the rules on questions in study material: null results, disputed findings, and practice that a study contradicts. Terms are defined in [frame](frame.md).

## Whether recall formats beat multiple choice

- The lab says yes: cued recall g = 0.61 against recognition 0.29 (Rowland 2014).
- Classrooms say no: recall 0.541 against recognition 0.607 when feedback follows (Yang 2021).
- Short answer was little or no better than multiple choice in three of four experiments, and better only when initial recall succeeded (Smith 2013c); quiz formats tied in one course (Lawson 2021).
- Yang 2021 reports format as a significant moderator in its results, Q(8) = 61.333, and in its summary says format does not significantly modulate the effect. The two statements were read in the same full text.
- A rule "prefer short answer" is therefore not supported in classrooms; a rule "use competitive options" has lab support only (Little 2012).

## Costs of multiple choice

- Reading lures plants false answers that persist a week (Roediger 2005; Fazio 2010); feedback reduces it (Butler 2008).
- The gain still outweighs the cost in the studies read (Marsh 2007), but none of them used flawed or test-bank items.
- Showing the stem before the options helped in three of four experiments, one in a classroom (van den Broek 2023), and not on a children's quiz site of 13,060 users (Brom 2026).

## Whether more feedback is better

- High-information feedback has the largest pooled effect (Wisniewski 2020), but heterogeneity is high, I2 = 83.4%.
- Immediate explanation feedback on demanding questions lowered effort and learning in one study of 59 students (Roelle 2016).
- A simpler correct-answer feedback beat more complex versions in a 1985 study by Kulhavy and colleagues, as Hattie 2007 reports it (read).
- Explanation feedback helped only new inference questions, not repeated ones (Butler 2013).
- Answer-until-correct equalled standard feedback (Butler 2007).

## Whether feedback should be delayed

- Single experiments favour delay (Butler 2007; Metcalfe 2009, for children).
- Classroom studies favoured immediate feedback (Kulik 1988, secondary), and the latest pool finds no average effect, g = 0.03 (Kandemir 2026, secondary).
- Learners prefer immediate feedback: 95.33% chose it (Lefevre 2016).
- A timing rule is not supported; the moderators (level, domain, time limits) are confounded by the authors' own account (Kandemir 2026).

## Whether higher-order questions always help

- Fact quizzes did not raise higher-order scores (Agarwal 2019), so a rule "facts first" lacks support; the reverse rule rests on few classroom studies (Jensen 2014: two sections of one course).
- Testing effects shrink with element interactivity (core: van Gog 2015, with the reply Karpicke 2015); this review did not find a study of question level with complex material that settles it.

## Weaknesses of the item-writing evidence

- Guidelines are textbook consensus: nearly half of 43 rules had no study (Haladyna 1989).
- Flaws had little effect on reliability (Downing 2005) and did not disadvantage borderline students (Tarrant 2008); effects on difficulty are inconsistent across flaws (Moore 2026).
- The three-option result measures tests, not learning (Rodriguez 2005).
- "None of the above" harmed only as a replacement for a good distractor (Pachai 2015).
- Most item-writing studies come from medical and nursing exams (Downing 2005; Tarrant 2006; Masters 2001).

## Whether peeking matters

- The evidence is old and read by abstract: programmed instruction (Anderson 1971) and a 1977 review (Kulhavy 1977).
- Covert answering makes retrieval invisible: a reader may peek without knowing it, and covert retrieval is weaker than overt (Yu 2025).
- No modern test of folded answers in a study text was found.

## Limits of the evidence on generated questions

- The positive comparisons come from authors working for the vendor of the system (Van Campenhout 2021; Johnson 2024), or measure item statistics only (Isley 2026).
- The negative ones are small samples: 24 doctors (Law 2025), 161 students (Laupichler 2023), 120 students (Chauhan 2025).
- Lu 2021b compares generated practice with no practice, so it shows a testing effect, not a parity with written questions.

## Limits of the practice evidence

- Owner's pages report their own reach: OpenStax 2026c (example only, scored in the instructional-text review) and Moodle 2026 (undated).
- The NBME guide was not opened; only its landing page was read (NBME 2026).
- Butler 2018 was read through its university's news release (WashU 2018), which states its recommendations without data.
- Q/A threads are opinion with no data, and several answerers cite the same two works (Haladyna 2002; Rodriguez 2005).

## Failed or missing replications

- This search found no replication of the higher-order transfer result of Jensen 2014 outside biology.
- It found no replication of Anderson 1971 on peeking in a modern medium.
- Kang 2007 and McDaniel 2007 on short answer against multiple choice were located by title only; their results are not claimed.
