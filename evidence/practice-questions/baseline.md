# Baseline: what the shapes are compared against

This file names the baseline that a proposed shape for questions in study material must beat, and says how the comparison would be measured. Terms are defined in [frame](frame.md).

## The named baselines

| Id | Baseline | Why this one |
|---|---|---|
| **B1**, to beat | a self-test of short-answer questions on recall, one per key idea, each followed by its correct answer once the learner has answered | the standard condition of the research: cued recall as the initial test gives g = 0.61 in the lab (Rowland 2014, to be checked in the paper), and feedback lifts classroom quizzes from 0.374 to 0.537 (Yang 2021) |
| **B0**, the practice control | end-of-chapter review questions as a textbook prints them: four-option multiple choice, mostly recall, answers away from the questions or absent | most adopted: 15 to 24 four-option items per chapter in Spielman 2020 (chapters 1 to 3); 93% of course items at the two lowest levels (Momsen 2010) |

- The shapes must beat B1: beating B0 is cheap, since B0 often lacks feedback and higher-order items, and B1 already supplies both repairs the evidence names.
- B0 stays in every comparison as the condition learners actually meet.
- The baseline sources are formal (Rowland 2014; Yang 2021; Momsen 2010) or a published textbook (Spielman 2020, 15 of 20 in sources.md).

## Candidate variant to test

- Questions at two levels per part: a recall question, then an application or inference question (Agarwal 2019; Pan 2018).
- Multiple-choice items, where used, with three competitive options built from known misconceptions (Little 2014; Rodriguez 2005; Sadler 1998), no "all" or "none of the above".
- Feedback that explains the key and each wrong option, not only names the key (Butler 2013; Wisniewski 2020).
- The answer folded beside the question, opened only after a written attempt; the fold is **convention, untested**.

## How a comparison would be measured

| Reader task | Outcome | Instrument | Prior study to borrow from |
|---|---|---|---|
| retain what was asked | proportion correct on the practised questions, reworded | delayed test, two days to a week | Butler 2010; Smith 2013c |
| transfer to new questions | proportion correct on new application and inference questions | items written before the study, never practised | Butler 2013; Pan 2018 (its transfer categories) |
| learn from wrong options | proportion correct on questions whose answers were distractors; rate of lure intrusions | cued-recall final test | Little 2014; Roediger 2005 |
| peek or not | share of answers opened before a written attempt; time to open | logged folds in a digital text | Anderson 1971 (peek condition); Brom 2026 (learner-paced delay) |
| cost | time on task; time to write the questions | logs; author timesheets | Law 2025 (24.5 against 96 person-hours) |

- Design: three arms (B0, B1, variant) between subjects, or a crossover over two chapters with counterbalanced order; same text and same time on task.
- Delay of at least two days before the final test, as in the cited studies.
- Effect sizes with confidence intervals, as Hedges' g; separate novices from learners with prior knowledge.
- Learning outcomes are kept apart from item statistics (difficulty, discrimination); the latter cannot stand in for the former (position.md).

## A checklist in the baseline's own terms

Per question, from B1:

1. It asks for one key idea of the part, in the learner's own words.
2. It can be answered without the text in view.
3. Its correct answer is given only after the learner has answered.

Per question, from item-writing guidance (Haladyna 2002; Brame 2013), when the item is multiple choice:

1. The stem states a full question.
2. Every option is plausible and comes from a common error.
3. No "all of the above", "none of the above" or combined options.
4. Options are parallel in grammar and length.
5. The stem avoids negation, or marks it.

A rule may be rejected deliberately; the rejection is written where a reviewer looks for the missing element.
